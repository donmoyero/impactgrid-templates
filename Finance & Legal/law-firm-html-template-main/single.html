The quiet rise of institutional arbitration in commercial disputes.

Why more boards are choosing arbitration over litigation — and the clauses that actually hold up under scrutiny.

Home/Insights/Article
Jan 12, 20266 min readBy Thomas Adler, Senior Partner

Institutional arbitration has become the default dispute-resolution mechanism for cross-border commercial contracts — and it is no longer only the reserved domain of multinationals. Mid-market companies, technology firms and even family offices are writing arbitration clauses into their commercial agreements at rates that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.

This shift is driven not by fashion but by experience. The cost and duration of commercial litigation in many jurisdictions — particularly in the United States, where discovery alone can consume months and six figures — have pushed boards towards alternatives that offer finality, confidentiality and a tribunal that understands the industry. Institutional arbitration, administered by bodies such as the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA) or the American Arbitration Association (AAA), provides the framework that makes those alternatives reliable.

The case for institutional arbitration

Ad hoc arbitration — where the parties and the tribunal agree the rules as they go — remains a viable option for two-party disputes with a single issue and a narrow remedy. But for the typical commercial dispute, involving multiple issues, multiple parties and multiple jurisdictions, the institutional framework provides three structural advantages.

First, procedural certainty. The institution's rules govern the timeline, the appointment of arbitrators, the exchange of evidence and the hearing. The parties know what they are committing to before the dispute arises. Second, quality control. Institutions vet the arbitrators on their panels, and they review awards before they are issued to ensure they are enforceable. Third, emergency relief. Most major institutions now offer emergency-arbitrator provisions that can grant interim relief within days — a feature that ad hoc arbitration cannot replicate without a court order.

Institutional arbitration is not a softer option than litigation. It is a different technology — one that trades public precedent for private efficiency, and the certainty of appeal for the finality of a binding award.

The data bears this out. The ICC reported a record 890 new cases in 2025, with an average amount in dispute of USD 54 million. The AAA's commercial division saw a 14 per cent increase in case filings over the same period. These are not small claims being diverted from small-claims court; these are material commercial disputes that would once have been filed in state or federal court.

Drafting the clause that works

The best arbitration clause is worthless if it is not properly drafted. Our disputes practice has reviewed hundreds of arbitration clauses in commercial agreements, and the same four issues arise with surprising frequency.

  • Scoping. The clause must define clearly which disputes are subject to arbitration — and which are not. A broadly worded clause that captures pre-contractual representations may inadvertently waive the right to bring a fraud claim in court.
  • Seat and law. The juridical seat of the arbitration determines the procedural law and the grounds for challenge. Choosing a neutral seat — London, Singapore, Geneva — avoids the perception of home-field advantage.
  • Number of arbitrators. A sole arbitrator is cheaper and faster. A three-member panel is more expensive but produces a more considered award and is harder to challenge. The clause should reflect the likely size and complexity of the dispute.
  • Cost allocation. The default rule in most institutions is that the loser pays the winner's costs. But the clause can modify this — for example, by providing that each side bears its own costs, or that the tribunal has a broad discretion to apportion them.

When arbitration does not fit

Institutional arbitration is not the right answer for every dispute. Cases that require public precedent — a test case, a declaratory judgment that binds third parties, or a matter involving a novel point of law — belong in court. So do cases where one party is likely to resist enforcement, because arbitration agreements are ultimately enforced by the courts, and a determined recalcitrant party can inflict significant delay.

Nor is arbitration necessarily cheaper than litigation in the short run. The arbitrator's fees, the institution's administrative charges and the cost of the hearing venue can exceed the filing fees and court costs of a trial. The cost advantage of arbitration is not that it is cheaper per hearing-day; it is that it is faster, more focused and therefore less expensive in total.

For mid-market companies, the calculus is increasingly clear. The institutional arbitration clause in a standard commercial contract costs nothing to include, and it preserves the option of a private, efficient, binding resolution if the deal turns sour. The clause that is not there cannot be negotiated after the dispute arrives.
